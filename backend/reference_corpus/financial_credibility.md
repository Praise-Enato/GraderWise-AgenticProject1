# Financial credibility in a business plan

Generic reference material for evaluating the financial section of ANY business
plan. Contains no applicant data — it describes what credible financials look
like and the red flags that signal error or fabrication.

## What credible financials contain
- **Startup / capital costs**, itemized: equipment, inventory, licences, deposits,
  working capital — not a single round number.
- **Revenue projections** with a stated basis: units × price, or customers ×
  average spend × frequency. A projection with no basis is a wish, not a forecast.
- **Expense / cost structure**: fixed costs (rent, salaries) vs variable costs
  (materials, commissions), and the main line items.
- **Profitability / break-even**: gross and net margin, and the point (in units,
  revenue, or months) where the business covers its costs.

## Internal-consistency checks (these must hold)
- Profit = Revenue − Expenses. Profit cannot exceed revenue.
- Margins must be arithmetically consistent with the stated revenue and costs.
- Totals in a table must equal the sum of their parts.
- Growth rates should be explainable, not simply "10× next year".
- Units, currency, and time periods must be stated and consistent.

## Red flags (score the affected criteria low)
- **Round, aspirational numbers** with no derivation ("we will make $1,000,000").
- **Impossible or contradictory figures** (profit > revenue; costs that don't sum;
  a margin that can't be produced by the stated numbers).
- **Hockey-stick projections** with no driver behind the inflection.
- **No costs at all** — revenue with no matching expenses.
- **Precision without basis** — false precision ($1,234,567) presented as certainty.

## Benchmarks (rough, context-dependent — use as sanity checks, not hard rules)
- Early-stage gross margins vary widely by industry: services high, physical
  goods lower. A plan claiming near-100% margin on a physical product is suspect.
- Customer-acquisition cost should be well below the lifetime value of a customer;
  a plan that ignores acquisition cost has not thought about unit economics.
- A plausible business reaches break-even within a stated, finite horizon; "never
  addresses break-even" is a gap, not a neutral omission.

## First-hand vs formal evidence
Evidence can be first-hand (the team's own sales records, customer counts, informal
surveys) or formal (external reports). First-hand, unsourced figures earn modest
partial credit — real but not the full marks reserved for well-evidenced claims.
Vague claims with no specifics earn little or nothing.
