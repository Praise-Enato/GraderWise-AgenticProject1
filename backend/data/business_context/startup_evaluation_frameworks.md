# Startup Evaluation Frameworks & Best Practices

## Y Combinator Startup Evaluation Criteria

Y Combinator evaluates startups on the following key dimensions:

### 1. Problem Clarity
- The best startups solve problems the founders have personally experienced.
- A strong problem statement is specific, measurable, and affects a clearly defined customer segment.
- Weak problem statements use vague language like "people struggle with..." without data.
- Benchmark: A good problem statement should pass the "hair on fire" test — is the customer actively seeking a solution right now?

### 2. Market Size
- YC looks for startups targeting markets of at least $1B TAM (Total Addressable Market).
- Bottom-up market sizing is preferred over top-down. Bottom-up: "There are 500,000 dentists in the US, each paying $500/month for scheduling software = $3B SAM." Top-down: "The healthcare IT market is $200B" is too vague.
- SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market) should be at least $100M for a venture-scale startup.
- SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) should show a realistic 1-5% capture in the first 3 years.

### 3. Founder-Market Fit
- Do the founders have unique insight or experience in this market?
- Have they talked to at least 50-100 potential customers?
- Do they have domain expertise or relevant technical skills?

### 4. Traction & Validation
- Pre-revenue: Letters of intent, waitlists, pilot customers, or design partnerships count.
- Revenue: Week-over-week growth rate of 5-7% is considered strong for early-stage startups.
- Monthly growth rates above 15-20% are excellent. Sustained monthly growth above 100% for more than 6 months is almost always unrealistic without extraordinary circumstances.

### 5. Defensibility
- Network effects, proprietary data, regulatory moats, switching costs, or brand are valid moats.
- "First mover advantage" is NOT a defensible moat by itself.
- Patents are generally weak moats for software startups.

## Paul Graham's Startup Principles
- "Make something people want" — the single most important thing.
- Launch early and iterate. A perfect product that launches late loses to a good product that launches early.
- Do things that don't scale initially — manual processes are fine for validating demand.
- Growth rate is the best measure of a startup's health: revenue, users, or engagement growth.

## Standard Pitch Deck Evaluation
A strong startup pitch deck follows this structure (10-15 slides):
1. Title slide (company name, one-line description, contact)
2. Problem (what pain point are you solving?)
3. Solution (how does your product solve it?)
4. Market opportunity (TAM/SAM/SOM with methodology)
5. Product demo / screenshots
6. Business model (how do you make money?)
7. Traction (revenue, users, growth metrics)
8. Competitive landscape (positioning matrix, not just a list)
9. Team (relevant experience, complementary skills)
10. Financial projections (3-5 year, realistic assumptions)
11. Ask (funding amount, use of funds, milestones)

### Common Pitch Deck Red Flags
- Claiming "no competition" — every startup has competition, even if indirect.
- Hockey-stick revenue projections without clear assumptions.
- No mention of CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) or customer acquisition strategy.
- Team slide listing impressive titles but no relevant domain experience.
- Asking for funding without specifying milestones or use of funds.
