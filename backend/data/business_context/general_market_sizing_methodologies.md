# Market Sizing Methodologies

## TAM, SAM, SOM Framework

### Total Addressable Market (TAM)
- The total global revenue opportunity if you captured 100% of the market.
- Used to show the ceiling of opportunity — investors want to see TAM of $1B+ for venture-scale startups.
- Should be calculated, not just cited from an analyst report.

### Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM)
- The portion of TAM you can realistically serve given your product, geography, and go-to-market strategy.
- Typically 10-40% of TAM depending on constraints.
- Example: TAM is global CRM market ($60B). SAM is CRM for small law firms in the US ($2B).

### Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)
- The portion of SAM you can realistically capture in the first 3-5 years.
- Typically 1-10% of SAM for a new entrant.
- This is the most scrutinized number — it must be grounded in achievable sales targets.
- Example: "We plan to capture 5% of the $2B US small law firm CRM market = $100M in year 5."

## Bottom-Up Market Sizing (Preferred)

### Methodology
1. Identify the specific customer segment.
2. Count the number of potential customers in that segment (use census data, industry reports, LinkedIn, trade associations).
3. Estimate annual revenue per customer (based on pricing model).
4. Multiply: Number of Customers x Revenue per Customer = Market Size.

### Example
- There are 200,000 independent dental practices in the US (ADA data).
- Each practice spends approximately $3,000/year on practice management software (industry survey).
- Bottom-up TAM = 200,000 x $3,000 = $600M.
- SAM (practices with 2-10 dentists, our target) = 80,000 practices = $240M.
- SOM (5% capture in 3 years) = 4,000 practices = $12M ARR.

### Why Bottom-Up is Preferred
- Shows the founder has done primary research and understands unit economics.
- Produces defensible, verifiable numbers.
- Demonstrates understanding of the specific customer segment.

## Top-Down Market Sizing (Weaker but acceptable as supplement)

### Methodology
1. Start with a large industry figure from an analyst report (Gartner, IDC, Statista).
2. Apply successive filters to narrow to your target segment.
3. Apply a market share assumption.

### Example
- Global project management software market: $6B (Gartner 2024).
- North America share: 40% = $2.4B.
- SMB segment: 30% = $720M.
- Our target (construction SMBs): 15% = $108M.

### Weaknesses of Top-Down
- Relies on analyst reports that may use different definitions.
- Easy to cherry-pick large numbers.
- Doesn't prove the founder understands the actual customer.
- Investors can easily challenge the percentage assumptions.

## Common Market Sizing Mistakes in Business Plans

### Red Flags
- Citing only TAM without SAM/SOM breakdown.
- Using top-down only without any bottom-up validation.
- Claiming TAM from a different market category (e.g., citing "global software market" for a niche HR tool).
- SOM projections that imply capturing >10% market share in year 1 — extremely rare for new entrants.
- No sources cited for market data.
- Using outdated market data (>3 years old).
- Confusing market size with revenue — "$10B market" should specify if that's revenue, transaction volume, or something else.

### Best Practices
- Always provide both bottom-up and top-down analysis, showing convergence.
- Cite specific, verifiable sources (government data, trade associations, published reports).
- Show your math — don't just state the number.
- Address market growth rate and trends.
- Acknowledge market concentration (are there dominant incumbents?).
- Define your market precisely — narrow is better than broad.

## Industry-Specific Market Size Benchmarks
- Global SaaS market: ~$200B (2024), growing at 12-15% CAGR.
- US EdTech market: ~$40B, growing at 16% CAGR.
- US HealthTech market: ~$150B, growing at 18% CAGR.
- US FinTech market: ~$120B, growing at 20% CAGR.
- Global e-commerce: ~$6T, growing at 10% CAGR.
- Minimum viable market size for venture-backed startup: $1B TAM.
- Minimum for a bootstrapped/profitable business: $10-50M SAM can be sufficient.
