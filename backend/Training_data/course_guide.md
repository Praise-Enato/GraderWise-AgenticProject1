# Business Plan Competition: Comprehensive Course Guide

This guide is designed to help you construct a compelling, thorough, and highly competitive business plan and pitch. Each section breaks down the specific expectations for your proposal and video presentation, explaining why each element matters and how to effectively execute it.

---

## Video - Contestant Face on Video

When pitching a business, investors are investing in *you* just as much as they are investing in your idea. Having your face clearly visible on video establishes a personal connection, builds trust, and allows the judges to read your body language and facial expressions. It demonstrates confidence and passion for your venture. A faceless voiceover over slides often feels disconnected and lacks the persuasive power needed to convince stakeholders to back your vision. Ensure your lighting is good, your camera is at eye level, and you make "eye contact" with the lens.

## Video - Video Between 4 and 5 Minutes

Time management is a critical entrepreneurial skill. Investors and judges review numerous pitches, and their time is highly valuable. A strict time limit forces you to distill your business plan down to its absolute most essential, compelling components. If your video is too short, you likely missed critical details or failed to prove your business's viability. If it is too long, it shows a lack of discipline and an inability to be concise. Practice your pacing to ensure you comfortably fit all necessary information within this precise window without rushing.

## Video - Quality of Content (Idea has merit, viable data documents demand and solution)

A great idea is not enough; it must be a viable business opportunity. This criterion evaluates the fundamental substance of your pitch. Does the market actually want this? You must present empirical, objective data—not just personal opinions or anecdotes—to prove that there is a genuine demand for your product or service. You need to show that your proposed solution technically and economically works. High-quality content means your pitch is rooted in reality, backed by research, and presents a logical, cohesive argument for why your business will succeed.

## Video - Speak Dynamically vs Reading Same Information on Slide

Your slides are a visual aid, not a teleprompter. Reading directly from your slides is redundant, boring, and wastes the visual medium. Your presentation should be dynamic—you should be telling a story and elaborating on the concepts presented on the screen. The text on your slides should be minimal, consisting of high-level bullet points, charts, or images. Your spoken words should provide the context, the passion, and the narrative. Engaging the audience with vocal variety and enthusiasm is key to a memorable pitch.

## Problem/Pain Point - Clearly Defined the Problem/Pain Being Addressed

Every successful business exists to solve a problem or relieve a "pain point" for its customers. If you do not clearly define the problem, the rest of your business plan is irrelevant because nobody will care about your solution. You must articulate exactly what the issue is, who experiences it, and why it is frustrating, costly, or time-consuming for them. The best problem definitions are highly relatable and often elicit a "nod of agreement" from the audience. Make the judges feel the pain before you introduce the cure.

## Problem/Pain Point - Outside Data Confirms the Problem is Huge (Scalable)

It is not enough to solve a problem for a small handful of people; for a business to be a viable investment, the problem must be widespread. You must use reputable, external data (industry reports, census data, credible surveys, market research) to quantify the magnitude of the problem. Proving that the problem is "huge" directly ties to your business's scalability. If millions of people or thousands of businesses experience this pain point, your venture has the runway to grow into a large, profitable enterprise.

## Problem/Pain Point - Examples of What Others Are Doing to Solve the Problem

No problem exists in a vacuum. People are currently trying to solve this pain point *somehow*, even if the current method is inefficient, expensive, or frustrating (often called the "status quo"). By highlighting how people currently cope with the problem, you demonstrate deep market awareness. It shows the judges that you have researched the landscape and understand the customer's current journey. This sets the stage perfectly for you to introduce how your solution disrupts this current behavior.

## Business Venture Product and Service - Describe How Your Solution is Better Than the Other Options

This is your Competitive Advantage or Unique Selling Proposition (USP). Now that you have established the problem and how it is currently being solved, you must explicitly state why your product or service is superior. Is it faster? Cheaper? Higher quality? More user-friendly? Does it use proprietary technology? You must clearly articulate the gap between the existing alternatives and your innovation, making it obvious why a consumer would switch from their current habit to your offering.

## Business Venture Product and Service - Describe How it Generates Revenue (Monetization)

A brilliant product is a hobby until it makes money; then it is a business. You must clearly explain your business model. How exactly will money change hands? Are you using a subscription model, a one-time direct sale, a freemium model, licensing, or taking a transaction fee? Explain your pricing strategy and why it makes sense for your target market. Judges need to see a clear, realistic path from customer acquisition to cash in the bank.

## Business Venture Product and Service - Describe Cost Structure

Understanding how you make money is only half the equation; you must also understand how you spend it. You need to outline both your fixed costs (rent, salaries, software subscriptions) and your variable costs (materials, shipping, manufacturing per unit). Demonstrating a firm grasp on your cost structure proves that you understand the underlying unit economics of your business. It allows judges to see at what point your business becomes profitable and how efficiently you can scale operations.

## Business Venture Product and Service - Explain Value Proposition

Your value proposition is the core promise you make to your customer. It is a clear, concise statement that summarizes why a consumer should buy your product or use your service. While "how your solution is better" focuses on the competition, the value proposition focuses on the *customer's outcome*. It bridges the gap between the features of your product and the direct benefits the user will experience (e.g., saving 10 hours a week, increasing their sales by 20%, or providing peace of mind).

## Market Size/Growth Potential - Analysis of the Target Market and Growth Trends

Investors want to board a moving train, not a stationary one. You must analyze the broader industry landscape. Is your target market expanding or shrinking? What are the current macroeconomic trends, technological shifts, or cultural changes driving this market? Provide metrics like the Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR). Showing that you are entering a growing market means that even if you only capture a small slice of the pie, that slice will naturally grow larger over time.

## Market Size/Growth Potential - Clearly Identify Who the Buyer Is

"Everyone" is not a target market. If you try to sell to everyone, you will effectively sell to no one. You must create specific, highly targeted customer personas. Who is the actual person pulling out their credit card? Are you B2B (Business-to-Business) or B2C (Business-to-Consumer)? Define them by demographics (age, location, income) and psychographics (values, interests, behaviors). Knowing exactly who your buyer is dictates how you build your product and how you market it.

## Market Size/Growth Potential - Identify Who Competitors Are

Never tell an investor that you have "no competition." If there is no competition, there is usually no market. Competition validates your idea. You must explicitly list your direct competitors (those solving the same problem with the same type of solution) and indirect competitors (those solving the same problem with a different approach). Naming your competitors shows confidence, thorough research, and a realistic understanding of the battlefield you are entering.

## Market Size/Growth Potential - Strengths and Weaknesses of Competitors

Once you have identified your competitors, you must analyze them objectively. What do they do exceptionally well? (Do they have a massive marketing budget? Brand loyalty?) More importantly, where are they failing? What are their blind spots, negative reviews, or technological limitations? By mapping out their strengths and weaknesses, you reveal the exact vulnerabilities in the market that your business is designed to exploit.

## Marketing and Sales Strategy - Plan to Acquire Customers

Having a great product does not guarantee that people will find it. You must outline a concrete Go-To-Market (GTM) strategy. How will you generate awareness and drive traffic? This involves detailing your marketing campaigns, whether it is through SEO, content marketing, paid social media advertising, influencer partnerships, or PR. You need to explain the journey a customer takes from never having heard of you to making their first purchase, and roughly what it will cost to acquire them (Customer Acquisition Cost - CAC).

## Marketing and Sales Strategy - Identify Sales Channels

A sales channel is the specific medium through which your product is delivered to the end consumer. Will you sell directly to consumers via your own e-commerce website? Will you use third-party marketplaces like Amazon? Will you sell through brick-and-mortar retail stores, or use a B2B direct sales team doing cold outreach? Clearly defining your sales channels explains the logistics of your revenue generation and the intermediaries involved.

## Marketing and Sales Strategy - Sales Forecasts Backed by Data

Projections show your ambition, but data shows your grounding in reality. You need to provide sales forecasts for the upcoming months/years, but they cannot be arbitrary numbers pulled from thin air. Your forecasts must be justified by your marketing strategy, market size, and current traction. For example, "If we spend X on marketing based on an industry-average conversion rate of Y%, we expect to acquire Z customers." Show the math behind your milestones.

## Marketing and Sales Strategy - Identified Risk Factors That Will Impact Sales Goals

Every business faces risks. Inexperienced entrepreneurs pretend risks do not exist; mature founders identify them and plan for them. What could go wrong? Could a shift in algorithms destroy your marketing channel? Could a supply chain delay prevent you from fulfilling orders? Are there looming regulatory changes? By proactively identifying these risk factors, you show judges that you are pragmatic and have contingency plans in place, making you a safer investment.

## Financials - Past 3 Years Provided

If your business is already operating, historical financial data is the ultimate proof of concept. Providing your past financials (Profit & Loss statements, revenue growth, cash flow) shows how you have managed money thus far. It provides a baseline to prove that your business model actually works in the real world. If you are a pre-revenue startup, this section might be adapted to show the history of your funding, grants, or the personal capital you have invested to get to this point.

## Financials - Forecast Profits and Expenses

This is your financial roadmap for the future (Pro Forma statements). You must project your expected revenues, gross margins, operating expenses, and net profit over the next 3 to 5 years. This shows the judges the ultimate financial potential of the company. It reveals when you expect to break even and when the company will become highly profitable. It also shows if you truly understand the scale of expenses required to grow your business.

## Financials - Detailed Breakdown

High-level summaries are not enough for the financial section; the devil is in the details. You must provide a granular breakdown of your numbers. Instead of just saying "Marketing: $50,000," break it down into exactly what that entails (e.g., $20k for ad spend, $15k for an agency, $15k for trade shows). This detailed breakdown allows judges to evaluate your assumptions. It proves that you have thought through every operational requirement of the business.

## Current Management Team - Depth of Expertise

Ideas are cheap; execution is everything. Investors invest in teams. Why are *you* and your co-founders the absolute best people in the world to build this specific business? You must highlight the relevant domain expertise, past successes, technical skills, and industry connections of your management team. If you are building medical software, having a doctor or healthcare compliance expert on your team provides massive credibility. Highlight what makes your team uniquely capable.

## Current Management Team - Demonstrate Leadership is Agile

Startups rarely end up exactly where they started. You will face unforeseen challenges, market shifts, and product failures. You must demonstrate that your leadership team is agile—meaning you are capable of learning quickly, pivoting when the data tells you to, and adapting to new realities without falling apart. Share brief examples of how you have overcome obstacles, adapted to feedback, or changed your approach based on new evidence. Coachability and resilience are key indicators of an agile team.

## Use of Prize Money - Specific Budget

If you win this competition, what exactly are you going to do with the funds? Investors and judges do not want to hand over money for "general operations" or to pay founders' salaries in the early days. They want to fund growth. You must provide a highly specific, itemized budget for the prize money. Explain exactly what milestones this specific amount of money will unlock (e.g., "The $10,000 will be used specifically to finalize the patent filing ($4k) and manufacture the first 500 beta units ($6k)"). Prove that the prize money will create a tangible ROI.

## Conclusion - Overall Summary

As you wrap up your pitch, you need to bring everything back together in a powerful elevator pitch. Remind the judges of the core narrative: the severe problem, your brilliant solution, and the massive market opportunity. The overall summary should leave no ambiguity about what your business does and why it matters. It is your final chance to cement your core message in their minds.

## Conclusion - Highlighted Key Points

While the summary is narrative, highlighting key points serves as a rapid-fire recap of your most impressive metrics and milestones. Did you secure a letter of intent? Do you have 500 people on a waitlist? Are your profit margins exceptionally high? Reiterate the 2 or 3 most impressive, unarguable facts from your presentation. This ensures the judges are thinking about your strongest assets as they fill out their rubrics.

## Conclusion - Link in Video to the Google Slide Deck

This is a critical logistical requirement. Judges often need to review your materials after the video is over to double-check data, review your financial breakdowns, or look closer at your product design. You must provide a clear, accessible, and functional link to your presentation slide deck (like a Google Slides link with the correct viewing permissions). Failing to provide this can result in judges missing key information that was glossed over in the fast-paced video.
